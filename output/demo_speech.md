Good [morning/afternoon], judges. We're Team QubitRoute, and we built a working answer to problem statement SIH26137 — quantum-inspired traffic route optimization.

Here's the real-world problem in one line: any delivery vehicle, ambulance, or service van that has to visit multiple stops in a city has to answer one question — in what order should I visit them? That sounds simple, but the number of possible orderings explodes so fast with every extra stop that even powerful computers can't just try them all. It's called the Traveling Salesman Problem, it's one of the most-studied problems in computer science, and it sits at the heart of every real logistics and dispatch system in the country.

What we've built is not a mockup or a slide-deck idea — it's a live, running application. You can open it right now at our deployed link, click anywhere on a real street map of any Indian city, drop a start point, an end point, and a handful of stops in between, and in seconds it computes the fastest order to visit them, drawn along real roads, accounting for what time of day it is.

So let me walk you through the pipeline — what actually happens between your click and that route appearing on screen.

**Step one** is the road network. When you click the map, the browser asks OSRM — a real open-source routing engine — for the actual road-network travel time between every pair of points you've placed. Not straight-line distance. Real roads, real turns.

**Step two** is congestion. A route that takes twenty minutes at midnight can take forty at rush hour, and a router that ignores that isn't actually solving the real problem. So before we optimize anything, we run that travel-time matrix through a time-of-day congestion model — rush-hour peaks around nine in the morning and six-thirty in the evening — so the route we choose is the one that's actually fastest at the hour you're traveling, not just the one that's shortest on paper.

**Step three** is where the "quantum-inspired" part comes in. We take the problem of finding the best visiting order and reformulate it as something called a QUBO — a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization — which is the exact mathematical language that quantum annealers, like D-Wave's hardware, are built to solve. We solve it here using simulated annealing, a classical algorithm that searches the same energy landscape a quantum annealer would. And — we want to be upfront about this, because judges will ask — we also have a version of this running against a real D-Wave quantum annealer over the cloud, and a second, completely different quantum computing paradigm called QAOA, a gate-based variational quantum circuit, running on the same formulation. So this isn't quantum-inspired in name only — the same problem genuinely ports to real quantum hardware, and to two separate quantum computing approaches, with zero reformulation.

**Step four** is honesty. We benchmarked our QUBO solver against classical nearest-neighbor plus 2-opt, and separately against Google OR-Tools' actual production routing solver. And we're going to tell you the real result instead of a convenient one: on plain routing, with no constraints, classical local search matches or beats our quantum-inspired solver, and that gap gets wider as the problem grows. That's not a bug — it's a well-known result in operations research, and pretending otherwise would fall apart the moment a judge tested it themselves.

But here's the actual finding, and it's the one we want you to remember: real dispatch is never just "shortest path." It always has business rules on top — a pickup that must happen before its drop-off, a stop with a hard cutoff, a vehicle that can't take a certain road. We added one such rule to the problem and ran fifteen trials. Classical 2-opt, which has no concept of the rule, violated it in eight of those fifteen trials — and patching the route afterward to fix each violation cost an average of almost nine percent extra travel time, and as much as forty-four percent in the worst case we measured. Our QUBO solver, where the rule is simply one more term in the same optimization, satisfied it in all fifteen out of fifteen — by construction, not by checking afterward. That's the real technical contribution here: as you stack more real-world constraints onto a route, a formulation where every new rule is just another penalty term scales cleanly, while patching a classical router after the fact gets more expensive with every rule you add.

And that same architecture already goes further than a single-vehicle demo. It handles multiple vehicles sharing a depot, with loads automatically balanced across them — by stop count, or by actual per-stop weight, like a real capacity constraint. It reacts live: if you simulate an accident on one leg of the route, it re-solves and re-routes around it in real time, not just relabels the same path. You can search for a place by typing, or just speak it — there's a voice-search microphone built on the browser's own speech recognition, no external API, no extra cost. And the whole thing is backed by two hundred and thirty-eight automated tests plus a real-browser layout suite, because we wanted every claim we're making up here to be something you could actually verify in our repository, not just something we're saying.

So, to summarize what you're about to see: a real road network, a live traffic model, a QUBO formulation that runs on classical hardware today and on real quantum hardware when you need it, benchmarked honestly against the best classical tools that exist, and a genuine, measured advantage the moment real-world constraints enter the picture — which is exactly where real logistics dispatch lives.
